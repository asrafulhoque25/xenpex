How to choose a website package for your small business

What a complete package includes

Most small business owners don't shop for websites often. The last time you bought one was probably five or more years ago, and the market has changed completely since then. Before you pay anyone — a freelancer, an agency, or a productized service like ours — here's what to look for and what to walk away from.

What a complete website package should actually include

The phrase "website package" gets used loosely. Some packages are a Wix template with $200 of design slapped on top. Others are six-figure custom builds. To compare apples to apples, here are the eight components a complete small business website package should contain.

Logo and brand identity. Not just a logo file — a usable brand system. Color palette with specific hex codes. Typography choices for headings and body. Logo variations: horizontal, stacked, monochrome, favicon. If the package doesn't include this, you'll end up with a great website wrapped in inconsistent branding that weakens every page it touches.

The four ways to buy

There are really only four ways to get a small business website built, and each trades cost against control. Knowing which one fits your situation saves you from paying agency prices for freelancer work, or freelancer prices for a template you could have bought yourself.

DIY builders vs. freelancers vs. agencies vs. productized services

DIY builders like Wix or Squarespace are the cheapest and fastest, but the result looks like everyone else's and you do all the work. A freelancer gives you a custom look at a fair price, though timelines and availability swing with their workload.

Agencies bring a full team and process, which you pay for in both budget and overhead. Productized services sit in the middle: a fixed scope, a fixed price, and a predictable timeline, with less flexibility than custom agency work.

Red flags to watch for

Most bad website experiences are predictable from the first conversation. The warning signs show up in how a vendor talks about scope, ownership, and what happens after launch — long before any code is written.

Signs to walk away from

Vague scope is the biggest one. If a quote doesn't list deliverables, page count, and revision limits, the gaps become change orders later. Watch for vendors who won't transfer ownership of the domain, hosting, or source code, or who lock you into a proprietary platform you can't leave.

No post-launch support, no written timeline, and prices that seem far below the market are also red flags. Cheap up front usually means expensive later, paid in your own time fixing what was skipped.

How to compare honestly

Comparing quotes by price alone is how people end up disappointed. A $1,500 site and a $9,000 site can both be "a website," but they rarely include the same things. Compare on deliverables, not on the number at the bottom.

Line up the scope, not the totals

Ask every vendor for an itemized scope: how many pages, what's custom versus template, who writes the copy, who provides images, how many revision rounds, and what's included for SEO and analytics. Put the answers side by side in a simple table.

Then check ownership and support terms, and ask to see two or three live examples in your industry. The cheapest quote with half the scope is not the cheapest quote.

Realistic timelines

A simple small business site is rarely a weekend job, and rarely a six-month one either. Knowing the real phases helps you spot a vendor who's over-promising speed or padding the schedule.

What each phase usually takes

Discovery and content gathering takes one to two weeks, and it's where most projects actually stall — usually waiting on your copy and photos. Design runs another one to two weeks, with a round or two of revisions.

Build and development is typically two to four weeks for a standard site, followed by testing and launch. A realistic end-to-end timeline for a small business site is four to eight weeks, assuming you turn feedback around quickly.

What you should own

When the project is done, the website should belong to you — not to the person who built it. Ownership is where a lot of small businesses get quietly trapped, and it only becomes obvious when they try to leave.

The assets that should be in your name

Your domain and hosting accounts should be registered under your email, with you as the admin. You should have the source code or full CMS access, your brand assets and original design files, and all the content and images on the site.

You should also own your analytics and any connected accounts. If switching vendors would mean rebuilding from scratch, you didn't actually own your website — you were renting it.

Questions to ask

A short list of direct questions tells you more about a vendor than any portfolio. The good ones answer clearly and in writing. The ones to avoid get vague, defensive, or change the subject to price.

Ask every vendor these before signing

What exactly is included, and what costs extra? Who owns the domain, hosting, and code when we're done? How many revision rounds do I get? What happens if I want to make changes after launch, and what does support cost?

Can I see live sites you've built in my industry? What's the realistic timeline, and what do you need from me to hit it? If the answers come back specific and in writing, you're talking to the right vendor.
